## 1a The warm back

[VISUAL: Kitchen-table theme, warm cream. DATE: TODAY. REASON: -1. A tall paper fridge cutout stands centre, door shut. A paper hand reaches round to its back, low down. At "It's warm.", soft red heat lines rise off the coils at the back.]

Reach behind your fridge, down near the bottom, and you will feel something odd. It's warm.

[VISUAL @ "The box that": The fridge becomes a cutaway: frosty blue inside, a milk bottle on a shelf, a small thermometer reading cold (blue). Red heat arrows run from the milk, through the cabinet, out of the back coils and into the kitchen. At "It moves heat.", the label HEAT lands on the arrow.]

The box that keeps your milk cold is pushing heat into your kitchen, day and night. A fridge doesn't make cold. It moves heat.

[VISUAL @ "For almost all": The fridge LIFTS off. Navy night. DATE: BEFORE 1755. At "carried down from a mountain", a snowy peak and a basket of snow sliding down its side. At "caught on a winter night", a thin sheet of ice forms on a shallow dish under stars. At "from nothing", both LIFT off, leaving an empty workbench.]

For almost all of history, that was impossible. Cold could only be gathered: carried down from a mountain, or caught on a winter night. It could not be made from nothing.

## 2a The ice

[VISUAL: Navy night. DATE: IRAN, 1600s. REASON: 0. A flat paper map of Iran slides in; the central desert shaded sand; pin YAZD pops in. At "forty degrees", a paper thermometer beside the map climbs to about 40 °C, past a small tick marked "HIGH FEVER 39 °C".]

Iran's central desert, in the sixteen hundreds. Summer afternoons here reach about forty degrees, hotter than a high fever. Yet in towns like Yazd, you could buy ice in high summer.

[VISUAL @ "It was caught": The map LIFTS off. Side view under a starry navy sky: a long shallow pool on the ground, a tall wall along its south side. A low winter sun sits behind the wall and the pool lies in its shadow. At "underground channel", water trickles in from a qanat: a dashed tunnel with a row of well shafts above it. At "as deep as your hand is wide", a paper hand measures the water.]

It was caught on winter nights. Water from an underground channel ran into a long pool, about as deep as your hand is wide. A tall wall kept the low winter sun off it.

[VISUAL @ "The cold came": Soft heat arrows rise from the water straight up, past the stars, to a label SPACE at the top of the frame. A small air thermometer beside the pool stays just above zero, label "AIR: ABOVE 0 °C". At "freeze", ice crystals spread across the pool. At "layer on layer", new thin layers stack up night after night (the stars wheel, three times), until a label reads "≈ 50 CM".]

The cold came from the sky. On a clear, still night, water sends its heat straight up into space, so it can freeze while the air stays above zero. Night after night, layer on layer, the ice grew half a metre thick.

[VISUAL @ "Workers cut it": The ice is cut into blocks that slide into a cross-section of a yakhchāl: a deep pit under a tall stepped mud-brick dome, a small paper figure beside it for scale, label "UP TO ≈ 18 M". At "an ice pit", the word YAKHCHĀL · "ICE PIT" appears under it. At "lasted into summer", DATE changes to SUMMER and one block leaves on a paper cart.]

Workers cut it into blocks and stacked it in a yakhchāl, an "ice pit": a deep hole under a mud-brick dome. The biggest stand as tall as a six-storey building. There the ice lasted into summer, and was sold.

## 2b Downhill

[VISUAL: Cream. DATE: ANY TIME. REASON: 0. The dome from 2a, small, dimmed at left. A paper hill fills the frame: HOT (red) at the top, COLD (blue) at the bottom. At "runs downhill", a red ball labelled HEAT rolls from HOT down to COLD. At "A cup of tea", a steaming mug sits in a cold blue room; its steam thins. At "It never warms itself", a reverse arrow (room to mug) appears and is crossed out.]

But the yakhchāl only caught the cold a clear night gave away. It could not make ice in summer.

Heat behaves like water on a hill. On its own, it only runs downhill, from hot to cold. A cup of tea cools in a cold room. It never warms itself from the air.

[VISUAL @ "To make cold": The mug LIFTS off. A paper hand pushes the HEAT ball back UP the hill; a label WORK appears on the hand. At "Remember that.", the ball holds at the top. Calm hold.]

To make cold on demand, you would have to push heat uphill. Remember that.

## 3a The trick

[VISUAL: Cream. DATE: GLASGOW, 1755. REASON: 1. Portrait: William Cullen, plate WILLIAM CULLEN · PROFESSOR OF MEDICINE, credit WELLCOME COLLECTION · CC BY 4.0. At "A thermometer", a thermometer dips into a small dish of spirit and lifts out; at "got colder", its red column drops.]

Glasgow, seventeen fifty-five. A pupil of the professor William Cullen noticed something odd. A thermometer dipped in spirit got colder as it dried.

[VISUAL @ "You have felt": The portrait LIFTS off. A paper swimmer steps out of a pool into the wind; small droplets lift off the skin as pale puffs, each carrying a little red heat arrow away. At "five times the heat", five small kettles (freezing to boiling) line up against one gram-sized droplet.]

You have felt it, stepping out of a pool on a windy day. To turn into vapour, a liquid needs energy, and it takes it as heat from whatever it touches. Evaporating a gram of water takes five times the heat of bringing it from freezing to boiling.

[VISUAL @ "So Cullen": The swimmer LIFTS off. A paper air pump beside a glass jar on a plate. Inside the jar, a small vessel labelled ETHER sits in a dish of water. At "pumped the air out", the pump handle strokes and small air dots leave the jar.]

So Cullen set a vessel of ether, a liquid that boils easily, in water inside a glass jar, and pumped the air out.

[VISUAL @ "Less air pressing": Side panel: a paper Everest with a kettle on its summit, a thermometer at 70 °C, a limp cup of tea. At "In Cullen's jar", the panel LIFTS off; the ether boils hard (pale puffs), red heat arrows run from the water into the ether.]

Less air pressing down means a liquid boils colder. On top of Everest, water boils at about seventy degrees, too cool for a good cup of tea. In Cullen's jar, the ether boiled at room temperature, and pulled heat out of the water around it.

[VISUAL @ "He wrote": Ice crystals spread through the dish and a white crust grows round the ether vessel. A paper quote card slides in: "we found the most part of the water frozen, and the vessel containing the aether surrounded with a thick and firm crust of ice." Source line: W. CULLEN, ESSAYS AND OBSERVATIONS, 1756.]

He wrote: "we found the most part of the water frozen, and the vessel containing the aether surrounded with a thick and firm crust of ice."

## 3b Gone into the air

[VISUAL: The quote card LIFTS off. The jar again: the ether level drops; its vapour puffs leave through the pump and drift off screen; a small label LOST. At "keep pouring in more", a paper bottle tips more ether in; the level drops again.]

But every drop of ether that boiled was sucked out through the pump and lost. To keep the cold coming, you had to keep pouring in more.

[VISUAL @ "Cullen never built": The jar slides onto a paper shelf. At "eighteen-oh-five", a ruler runs 1755 → 1805 with a faint pencil sketch of a machine at 1805 (label: DESCRIBED, NEVER BUILT); it then extends to 1834. Calm hold.]

Cullen never built a machine. In eighteen-oh-five, an American described one in a book. He never built it.

## 4a The loop

[VISUAL: Cream. DATE: ENGLAND, 1834. REASON: 2. Portrait: Jacob Perkins, plate JACOB PERKINS · AMERICAN INVENTOR. A small patent scroll beside it: "PATENT No. 6662 · 1834". At "Catch the vapour", the Cullen jar from 3b returns small; its escaping puffs are caught by a pipe that bends round into a closed ring.]

England, eighteen thirty-four. Jacob Perkins, an American inventor, patented a way to stop losing the liquid. Catch the vapour, squeeze it back into a liquid, and send it round again.

[VISUAL @ "Your fridge runs": Portrait and scroll LIFT off. A large paper diagram: a cabinet outline (left) and a closed loop of pipe running inside it and out of its back. The four parts appear ONE AT A TIME on their words in the next paragraphs; until then the pipe is empty. Keep the heat/cold colour code.]

Your fridge runs on that same loop. It has four parts.

[VISUAL @ "Inside the box": The EVAPORATOR coil inside the cabinet lights, label EVAPORATOR. Blue drops boil into pale puffs; red heat arrows flow from a milk bottle into the coil.]

Inside the box, the liquid boils at low pressure and soaks up heat from your food. That is the evaporator.

[VISUAL @ "From there, a pump": The COMPRESSOR (a paper piston in a cylinder, under the cabinet) appears, label COMPRESSOR. The piston pushes; the pale puffs crowd together and turn red. At "a bicycle pump", a small bicycle pump cutout beside it, its end glowing red.]

From there, a pump called the compressor squeezes the vapour. Squeezing a gas heats it up, the way a bicycle pump grows warm in your hand.

[VISUAL @ "Now hotter": The CONDENSER coil on the back of the cabinet appears, label CONDENSER. Red heat arrows flow out of it into the kitchen side of the frame; red puffs shrink back into drops.]

Now hotter than your kitchen, it runs through coils outside, the condenser. Its heat flows downhill into the room, and it turns back to liquid.

[VISUAL @ "Last, it squeezes": A narrow tube between condenser and evaporator, label EXPANSION. Drops squeeze through it one by one and turn blue; a small pressure gauge needle drops. Then the whole loop runs: drops and puffs circulate continuously.]

Last, it squeezes through a narrow tube. The pressure drops, and it goes cold again.

[VISUAL @ "That is the push": The paper hill from 2b slides in small at the top-right of the diagram; the compressor's piston pushes the HEAT ball up it, label WORK. The loop keeps running. Calm hold.]

That is the push uphill.

## 4b Behind the fridge

[VISUAL: Kitchen-table theme. DATE: TODAY. REASON: 2. The paper fridge from 1a slides back in; the loop from 4a is drawn faintly inside it. The back coils glow; red heat lines rise. Two small labels stack beside them: HEAT FROM YOUR FOOD, then (at "plus the work of the pump") + WORK OF THE PUMP.]

Now, back to your kitchen. That warmth behind your fridge is the heat taken out of your milk, plus the work of the pump.

[VISUAL @ "His patent promised": The fridge LIFTS off. Cream. DATE: 1835. A paper model machine (pipes, a hand crank, a small box) labelled HAGUE'S MODEL · 1835. At "nobody bought one", a plain mono label NO BUYERS appears under it.]

His patent promised a machine for making ice. An engineer, John Hague, built a working model. Nobody bought one.

[VISUAL @ "The loop was right": The model LIFTS off. At "eighteen-fifties", DATE: 1850s and a small paper ice machine gets a SOLD tag. At "The trouble was", theme flips to navy, DATE: 1870s, REASON: 3. Three paper bottles land one per name: AMMONIA, SULFUR DIOXIDE, METHYL CHLORIDE. At "all three were poison", a small skull label lands on each bottle, left to right. Calm hold to the end.]

The loop was right. In the eighteen-fifties, it finally sold. The trouble was what came to run inside it: ammonia, sulfur dioxide and methyl chloride. All three were poison.
