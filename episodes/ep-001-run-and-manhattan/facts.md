# Facts: The Tiny Island the Dutch Traded for Manhattan (ep-001)

Fact base for the script. The writer may use only what is here. Researched 2026-11-16.

**Confidence key:** **solid** = several reputable sources agree; **contested** = sources disagree or the figure rests on
one secondary source; **popular-myth** = widely repeated but false, or not backed by any source found.

**Source quality notes**
- Wikipedia articles are used where they cite scholarship; the underlying scholar is named (Loth 1995, Corn 1998,
  Straver 2018, Lape 2000, Boxer 1969, Lesaffer 2016, Burnett 2013). For a citable line on screen, name the scholar.
- Giles Milton, *Nathaniel's Nutmeg* (1999), is **popular history**. Its vivid figures (prices, James's title) could
  not be traced to a primary source. Do not present them as fact without hedging ("one popular history claims...").
- Charles Corn, *The Scents of Eden* (1998), is also popular history but is widely cited by scholars on 1621.
- Britannica pages returned HTTP 403 to the fetcher, so no Britannica figures are quoted below. Search snippets of
  Britannica's Banda article give "nine islands, about 44 sq km", which conflicts with Wikipedia (about 172 km²). Do not
  quote a total area for the group.
- Ignore all "LLMpedia" (llmpedia.scads.ai) and cavac.at pages. They are machine-generated and conflict with real sources.

## Red flags (popular claims to avoid or correct)

1. **"The Dutch traded Manhattan for Run."** False as worded. Breda (31 July 1667) worked on *uti possidetis*: each side
   kept what it held. England had taken New Amsterdam by force in 1664, and the Dutch had held Run since 1620/21. Nothing
   changed hands. What was "traded" was the **claims**: England dropped its claim to Run, and the Dutch dropped theirs to
   New Netherland. The Dutch also kept Suriname. *Accurate version:* "Each side kept what it had stolen from the other,
   and the paperwork made it permanent."
2. **"Nutmeg cured the plague" / "the only certain cure".** No evidence it worked. It does appear as one of about nine
   ingredients in early modern plague drinks (Sarah Longe's receipt book, c. 1610, Folger). The "only certain cure" line
   comes from Milton's popular history. *Accurate version:* "Doctors put it in plague remedies. It didn't work."
3. **"Nutmeg was worth more than its weight in gold."** No source compares nutmeg and gold prices. Milton calls nutmeg
   "as sought after as gold" (desirability, not price). *Accurate version:* "It sold in Europe for over a hundred times
   what it cost in Banda" (Jordan's 122:1 figure, attributed).
4. **"A 60,000 percent markup."** It traces to Milton and is repeated by AramcoWorld. Sources found range from 14× to
   60,000%. Use it only with attribution, or use "more than a hundredfold".
5. **"Manhattan was bought for $24 of beads."** The source says 60 guilders' worth of trade goods. The $24 conversion
   dates from 1844 (Brodhead). Beads are not in the source. The Lenape probably did not see it as a permanent sale. No
   deed survives.
6. **"James I was King of ... Puloway and Puloroon."** It could not be traced to any primary source; it appears to come
   from Milton. Do not assert it.
7. **"Courthope held out for 1,540 days."** That does not fit Dec 1616 to Oct 1620 (about 1,400 days). Say "nearly four
   years".
8. **"Run is 13 km²."** The English Wikipedia infobox figure conflicts with the 3 × 1 km dimensions. Use the dimensions.
9. **"Courthope landed on Christmas Day 1616."** This is the usual date, but a contemporary letter gives 13–14 December.
   Say "December 1616".
10. **"Exactly 44 orang kaya were executed."** Sources give 24, 40, 44 and 48. Say "dozens", or name the source.
11. **FAO "nutmeg" production rankings showing Guatemala first.** That category includes cardamom. It is not about nutmeg.
12. **"Banda to Malacca, 2,000 km."** It is more than 3,000 km in a straight line.
13. **"Manhattan was captured in August 1664."** The fleet arrived in late August. The surrender was 8 Sept 1664 (New
    Style), or late Aug/early Sept in English Old Style. Say "1664".

## Best story beats

1. **One fruit, two spices, one place.** Nutmeg and mace come from the same fruit (seed and red aril), and for centuries
   it all grew on a few volcanic islands about 20 km across. (§1.11–1.12)
2. **December 1616: an island gives itself to a king it has never seen.** Run's people agree to "surrender their island
   of Puloroon to our sovereign the king of England" (Spurway letter, Kerr's *Voyages*). (§4.3–4.4)
3. **39 men, no springs, nearly four years.** Courthope's garrison holds Run against the VOC. In January 1620 London
   votes him £100 a year. He never learns of it: c. 20 October 1620 he "received a shot in the breast", leapt overboard
   and was never seen again (DNB 1887). (§4.6–4.12)
4. **Coen's plan in his own words.** "...once again subjugate Banda, and populate it with other people" (letter, 26 Oct
   1620, via Straver 2018). His motto: "Despair not, spare not your enemies." (§5.2–5.3)
5. **8 May 1621: Japanese swordsmen behead the orang kaya.** About 15,000 Bandanese become about 1,000. The land is cut
   into 68 plantations worked by enslaved people. (§5.6–5.12)
6. **Every nutmeg tree on Run is cut down.** The Dutch destroy the very thing the English came for, and leave cattle
   roaming the empty island. (§5.14)
7. **60 guilders vs 7,246 beaver pelts.** The letter announcing Manhattan's purchase travelled with furs worth about
   1,000 times the island's price (Schaghen, 5 Nov 1626). (§6.4–6.5)
8. **The twist at Breda.** The Dutch had just burned the English fleet in the Medway (June 1667) and still "lost"
   Manhattan, because nobody traded anything: each side kept what it held. A century later a French smuggler (Poivre's
   man Provost) and two British invasions (1796, 1810) spread nutmeg worldwide, and today it sits on Grenada's flag.
   (§7, §8)

**Named humans to build the story around (max 4)**
- **Nathaniel Courthope** (c. 1585–1620): the English holdout on Run.
- **Jan Pieterszoon Coen** (1587–1629): the VOC governor-general who conquered Banda in 1621.
- **Peter Stuyvesant** (or Richard Nicolls): the man who gave up New Amsterdam in 1664. Stuyvesant gives a face to the
  Dutch side.
- **Pierre Poivre** (1719–1786): the French intendant whose smugglers broke the monopoly. (His death date is given as
  1786 in Wikipedia's lead; the body text conflicts. Verify before showing.)
- Alternative for a Bandanese voice: the orang kaya as a group. No individual Bandanese leader's name was verified in
  this pass.
